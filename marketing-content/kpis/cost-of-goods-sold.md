# Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)

**URL:** `/kpis/cost-of-goods-sold`
**Meta title:** Cost of Goods Sold: What Counts, What Doesn't | The Good Plans Co
**Meta description:** COGS is the direct cost of delivering your product or service. What belongs in COGS, what doesn't, and why most owners get this line wrong.

## Definition

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) is the direct cost of producing the revenue your business earned. If you didn't take the job, you wouldn't have spent the money — that's what makes it COGS.

## What counts as COGS

- **Materials and parts** consumed in delivering the product or service
- **Direct labor** for billable hours, including payroll burden (taxes, workers' comp, benefits attributable to that labor)
- **Subcontracted work** tied to specific jobs
- **Equipment rental** for a specific job (not equipment you own and use across jobs — that's overhead)
- **Job-specific costs:** permits, disposal, project-specific insurance, freight on materials
- **Direct supplies:** items consumed in delivering the service (cleaning chemicals for a cleaning company, paint for a painter)

## What does NOT count as COGS

This is where most owners get it wrong.

- **Your salary.** Owner pay is overhead, not COGS.
- **Office staff.** Receptionist, bookkeeper, administrator — overhead.
- **Office rent.** Overhead.
- **Marketing.** Overhead.
- **General insurance.** Overhead. (Job-specific insurance is COGS; the umbrella policy isn't.)
- **Trucks and equipment you own.** The depreciation is overhead, not COGS. You'd own these whether or not you took today's job.
- **Software subscriptions.** Overhead.
- **General-purpose fuel.** Overhead. (Fuel directly tied to a single job CAN be COGS for some businesses.)
- **Owner draws.** Not COGS, not even an operating expense. Draws are below operating profit.

## Why this distinction matters

Putting overhead in COGS makes your gross margin look worse than it is and prevents you from seeing what's actually happening.

Example: an owner reports a 22% gross margin and panics. We pull the COGS detail. They've been categorizing their salary, their office staff salaries, and their truck loans as COGS. After we move those to where they belong (overhead), the actual gross margin is 38%. The business isn't broken; the books were misleading.

The reverse mistake also happens: putting direct costs in overhead makes gross margin look artificially high, hiding the fact that pricing isn't sustainable.

Either way, the wrong categorization means you're making decisions on bad data.

## How to fix it

Most accounting setups (QuickBooks, Xero, Wave) let you reassign accounts between COGS and operating expenses. If you suspect yours is wrong, the fix is usually a one-time cleanup:

1. Print your last 12 months of COGS detail
2. Walk every line and ask: "Did this expense exist BECAUSE of a job?"
3. If yes, it's COGS. If no, it's overhead.
4. Reclassify and re-run your financials

This is the kind of cleanup an experienced bookkeeper or your accountant can do for you in a few hours. It's worth it. You can't manage what you can't see clearly.
