# Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)

**Meta description:** Customer Lifetime Value is what a customer is worth to your business over the entire relationship. The number that justifies what you can spend to acquire them.

## Definition

Customer Lifetime Value (LTV) is the total revenue (or sometimes profit) you can expect from a single customer over the entire time they do business with you.

It's the answer to: "If I land this customer, what are they worth to me, in total, by the time they leave?"

## Formula

The simple version:

```
LTV = Average Ticket × Repeat Rate × Average Retention (in transactions or years)
```

A more rigorous version uses gross profit instead of revenue, because LTV in profit dollars is what really tells you what you can afford to spend on acquisition:

```
LTV (profit) = (Average Ticket × Gross Margin %) × Average Number of Transactions Over Customer Lifetime
```

For most small businesses, the simpler revenue version is enough. The point isn't precision; it's seeing whether the customer relationship is structurally worth pursuing.

## What it tells you

LTV anchors three things:

1. **How much you can spend to acquire a customer.** LTV ÷ CAC = 3:1 is a healthy benchmark. If a customer is worth $3,000 to you over their lifetime and acquiring them costs $300, the marketing is working. If they're worth $400 and you're spending $300 to land them, you're spinning your wheels.

2. **Whether retention or acquisition is the right focus.** If LTV is low because customers leave after one transaction, working on retention will move the needle more than chasing new acquisitions.

3. **Where the real money is in your business model.** Some businesses make their money on first transaction (single big-ticket sales). Some make it over 5 years of repeat purchases. LTV tells you which one you're running.

## Common mistakes

- **Calculating LTV as revenue when you should use profit.** Revenue LTV is useful but inflates the number. Profit LTV is what tells you what you can actually spend.
- **Using an industry-wide average.** Your business has its own LTV. Don't assume yours matches a benchmark.
- **Not segmenting customer types.** Different customer segments have wildly different LTVs. A maintenance customer might be 10× the LTV of a one-time emergency customer. Averaging them hides the insight.
- **Treating LTV as static.** Customers' LTV changes as your service mix, pricing, and retention evolve. Recalculate it annually.

## How to improve it

Three reliable levers:

**1. Increase retention.**
Get more transactions per customer over time. A customer who comes back twice has 2× the LTV of a customer who comes back once. Loyalty programs, scheduled check-ins, maintenance contracts, retainers — all retention plays.

**2. Increase average ticket.**
Larger transactions on the same customer count means higher LTV. See [Average Ticket](/kpis/average-ticket) for the levers.

**3. Improve gross margin.**
If you're measuring LTV in profit dollars, higher margin means higher LTV with no other changes.

## What LTV reveals about strategy

For most small business owners, LTV exposes a strategy question: are you running a one-and-done business, or a repeat-customer business?

- **One-and-done:** every transaction is essentially with a new customer. LTV ≈ average ticket × gross margin. You'd live or die on acquisition. Marketing is the most important function.
- **Repeat-customer:** customers come back, refer others, sign up for ongoing service. LTV is much higher than per-transaction value. Marketing matters, but RETENTION and SERVICE QUALITY matter more.

Most small businesses can become repeat-customer businesses with intentional effort. Most don't because nobody's tracking LTV or retention rate, so the insight stays invisible.
