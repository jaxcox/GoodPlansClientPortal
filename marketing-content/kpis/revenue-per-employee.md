# Revenue Per Employee

**URL:** `/kpis/revenue-per-employee`
**Meta title:** Revenue Per Employee: A Simple Efficiency Benchmark | The Good Plans Co
**Meta description:** Revenue per employee is the simplest way to compare your business efficiency to others in your industry. Here's what it tells you and how to move it.

---

## Definition

Revenue per employee is total revenue divided by the number of full-time-equivalent (FTE) employees in your business, including yourself.

It's a rough measure of how much output your team is producing per person.

## Formula

```
Revenue Per Employee = Annual Revenue ÷ Total FTEs
```

Count yourself. Count part-timers as fractional FTEs (a 20-hour-per-week employee is 0.5 FTE). Include subs only if they're functionally part of your team (not project-specific).

## What it tells you

Revenue per employee is a high-level efficiency metric. It rolls up everything that happens in your business into one number: pricing, productivity, service mix, capacity, sales.

The number is most useful for two things:

1. **Comparing your business to others in your space.** Most industries have a typical range. If you're well below, you have efficiency room; if you're well above, you've probably built a strong operation.
2. **Tracking your own trend over time.** A business growing revenue per employee is getting more leverage per person. A business with flat revenue per employee while headcount grows is hiring without scaling.

Healthy ranges vary wildly:

- Trade businesses with crews: $150K-$300K per employee
- Service businesses (light overhead): $200K-$400K per employee
- High-margin professional services: $300K-$600K+ per employee
- Software/SaaS: often $250K-$1M+ per employee (different business model entirely)

These are starting points, not standards. Your business has its own healthy number.

## What it doesn't tell you

Revenue per employee is a HIGH-LEVEL metric. It hides a lot.

- It doesn't tell you which roles are over- or under-staffed
- It doesn't tell you whether the revenue is profitable
- It doesn't account for working hours, intensity, or burnout
- It compares poorly across businesses with very different margin structures

Use it for orientation, not for diagnosis. To diagnose what's actually happening, look at the more granular KPIs (labor efficiency, labor utilization, margin by service line).

## Common mistakes

- **Not counting yourself.** As the owner, you're part of the team. If you exclude yourself, the number is artificially inflated.
- **Forgetting part-timers and subs.** Skipping them inflates the per-FTE number too.
- **Tracking only the dollar number.** The trend matters more than the absolute. If revenue per employee is $200K but TRENDING DOWN, that's the signal.
- **Comparing across very different business types.** A salon and a software company can't be compared meaningfully on this metric.

## How to improve it

Three main levers:

**1. Lift average ticket.**
More revenue per transaction, same team size → higher revenue per employee. See [Average Ticket](/kpis/average-ticket).

**2. Improve close rate.**
More wins from the same lead volume → more revenue, same headcount.

**3. Fix underperforming roles.**
Some roles in some businesses produce more revenue per hour than others. If you have a role consistently under-producing, training or restructuring is the lever.

The lever most owners reach for first — "hire more people" — usually LOWERS revenue per employee in the short term. It's not always the wrong move, but it's worth asking whether you have an efficiency problem before you have a headcount problem.
