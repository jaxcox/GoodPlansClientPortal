# Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)

## Definition

Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC) is the total cost of acquiring one new customer. It includes everything you spent to get them in the door — marketing, advertising, sales effort, referral incentives, time-related costs.

## Formula

```
CAC = Total Acquisition Spend ÷ Number of New Customers Acquired
```

Run this monthly or quarterly. Annual CAC is too coarse to be useful for operational decisions.

"Total Acquisition Spend" should include:

- Ad spend (Google, Facebook, local)
- SEO costs and content production
- Marketing agency fees
- Sales salary or commission attributable to new customer acquisition (not retention work)
- Referral bonuses or incentive programs
- Networking, events, trade shows
- Branded materials (yard signs, vehicle wraps, etc.) amortized

Not everything you spend on marketing is acquisition — some is retention or general brand. For most small businesses with informal marketing, the simplification is "total marketing spend ÷ new customers." Imperfect but useful.

## What it tells you

CAC tells you whether your marketing is structurally viable. If you spend $400 to acquire a customer whose lifetime value to you is $1,200, that's healthy. If you spend $400 to acquire a customer whose lifetime value is $350, you're losing money on every new customer, regardless of how busy the business looks.

The full health check is: **CAC vs. Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)**. The ratio is what matters.

- LTV:CAC of 3:1 or better → healthy
- LTV:CAC of 1:1 to 3:1 → marginal, depending on payback period
- LTV:CAC below 1:1 → losing money on every customer

## Common mistakes

- **Only counting paid advertising.** If you ignore the time your team spends on sales, networking, or follow-up, your CAC looks lower than it is.
- **Not tracking which channels acquire which customers.** Aggregate CAC is okay; CAC by channel is much more useful. Different channels have very different economics.
- **Ignoring the time component.** A customer who takes 6 months to close has a different CAC than one who closes in 3 weeks, even if the dollar spend is the same. Sales cycle time matters.
- **Forgetting that "free" referrals aren't free.** Referral programs with incentives have a cost. Word-of-mouth referrals look free but are earned through service quality, which has costs upstream.

## How to improve it

Three reliable ways to lower CAC:

**1. Improve close rate.**
If you're sending estimates at $30 each in ad cost and your close rate is 25%, your CAC is $120. Lift close rate to 35% and your CAC drops to $86. Same marketing, cheaper customer acquisition.

**2. Increase referral rate.**
Referrals are usually the lowest-CAC channel. A formal program (or just consistent ask) can double the referral rate for most businesses. That dilutes the more expensive paid channels in your blended CAC.

**3. Optimize your channels.**
If you're spending money on a channel that doesn't produce, kill it. If you're spending equally across channels but one converts 3× better, shift the budget. Most small business marketing spend is allocated by habit, not by performance.

## When to ignore CAC

If your business is below ~$500K in annual revenue and growing on word-of-mouth + a small marketing budget, CAC is often not the most useful number to optimize. Volume of leads is. Once marketing spend is non-trivial (5%+ of revenue), CAC becomes a real lever.
