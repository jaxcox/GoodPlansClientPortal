# Gross Margin

**Meta description:** Gross margin is the percentage of revenue you keep after direct costs. Definition, formula, what it tells you, and the common mistakes owners make tracking it.

---

## Definition

Gross margin is the percentage of revenue that remains after subtracting the direct costs of delivering your product or service. It tells you how much of every dollar of revenue is left over to cover overhead, taxes, and profit.

## Formula

```
Gross Margin % = (Revenue − Cost of Goods Sold) ÷ Revenue × 100
```

Cost of Goods Sold (COGS) includes only the DIRECT costs of producing the revenue — materials, direct labor, subcontractors, items consumed in delivering the service. It does NOT include overhead like rent, marketing, office staff, or insurance.

## What it tells you

Gross margin is your pricing-and-cost barometer. When it's healthy and stable, your pricing is right and your direct cost structure is under control.

When it slides, one of three things is happening:

- Your direct costs (materials, labor, subs) have gone up but pricing hasn't followed
- You're winning lower-margin work that's pulling the blend down
- A specific cost line has crept up without anyone noticing

A healthy weekly margin trend looks flat or slowly rising. Choppy or sliding margin is a signal worth investigating.

## Common mistakes

- **Including overhead in COGS.** Office rent, marketing, and your salary are NOT direct costs. Mixing them in makes the number lower than reality and prevents you from comparing service lines.
- **Looking at it once a year instead of weekly.** Your CPA shows you gross margin annually. By then, anything you'd want to fix has already happened. The version that actually changes outcomes is the weekly version.
- **Confusing it with net margin.** Net (or operating) margin is what's left after overhead. Gross margin is what's left after direct costs only. They tell different stories.
- **Not breaking it down by service line.** A blended margin can hide unprofitable lines. If one service is profitable and another is bleeding, the average looks fine but the bleed is real.

## How to improve it

The most common levers:

1. **Review pricing.** If you haven't touched it in 18+ months, your margin is almost certainly lower than it should be.
2. **Tighten markup on materials or parts.** If you're charging cost plus a thin layer, you're underpricing the work of sourcing, stocking, and standing behind those items.
3. **Shift service mix.** Some services have structurally higher margins. Lean toward them.
4. **Stop discounting "to stay busy."** A 10% discount on a 35% margin job cuts your gross profit by almost a third.
5. **Track per-job or per-service margin.** Find the bleeders. Stop taking the work that loses money.
