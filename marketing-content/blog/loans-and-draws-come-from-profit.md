# Your loans and owner draws come from profit. If profit's negative, you're digging.

**URL:** `/blog/loans-and-draws-come-from-profit`
**Meta title:** Owner Draws and Loan Payments Come From Profit | The Good Plans Co
**Meta description:** Most small business owners don't realize that loan payments and owner draws come out of profit. If profit isn't positive, every draw is making the hole deeper.

---

## The thing nobody told you in business school (or anywhere)

If you take an owner's draw this month, where does that money come from?

Most owners I ask answer some version of "the bank account." Which is technically true. But it doesn't answer the more important question, which is:

**What gets reduced when you take that draw?**

The answer is profit. Not revenue. Not expenses. Profit. Same with loan payments, owner pay (if you're S-corp), distributions, and anything else that flows out of the business to you personally.

Most owners don't see this until I draw it out for them on a financial statement. Then it clicks, sometimes painfully.

## How the math actually works

A simplified version of how money flows through your business:

```
Revenue
  − Cost of Goods Sold (direct costs)
  = Gross Profit
  − Operating Expenses (rent, marketing, office salaries, etc.)
  = Operating Profit (this is what you actually have to work with)
  − Interest and Taxes
  = Net Profit
  − Owner Draws, Loan Principal Payments, Distributions
  = What's left to retain in the business
```

The line that matters: owner draws and loan principal payments come out of profit. They don't come out of revenue. They don't come out of expenses. They come out of profit.

## The trap that catches owners

The reason this trips people up: revenue is loud and profit is quiet. You see revenue coming in every day. You see invoices, jobs closing, deposits hitting. You don't see profit until somebody runs the math.

When the bank account is full and revenue is up, it feels like you can take a draw without consequences. Sometimes you can. But sometimes you can't, and the only way to know is by looking at profit, not revenue.

If profit is negative — even just for this month, even just for this quarter — and you take a draw, that money is coming from somewhere else. The somewhere else is usually:

- Reserves from prior profitable months
- Debt (you're borrowing to pay yourself, structurally)
- Unpaid taxes that are still in the bank but already owe to the IRS
- Vendor payments you're stretching

In all four cases, you're digging a bigger hole. The business looks fine on the surface because the bank account hasn't collapsed yet. But the math is getting worse every month.

## The owner who never sees it coming

The pattern I've seen play out:

- Year one: business is profitable. Owner takes appropriate draws. Bank account grows.
- Year two: business has a few rough months. Owner keeps drawing at the same level (the household needs the money). Bank account stays flat.
- Year three: business is meaningfully less profitable. Owner draws against tax money that's still in the account. Bank account looks okay, but the unpaid tax bill is building.
- Year four: tax bill hits. There's not enough money. Owner takes a personal loan or sells assets to pay it. The business has been operating at a structural loss for 18 months but nobody saw it.

This is preventable. It's preventable by separating money before it gets spent and by knowing your profit position every month, not just at year-end.

## What to do about it

Two things, in priority order:

**1. Know your profit, not just your revenue.**

Pull your operating profit number every month. Compare it to your draws and loan payments. If profit is less than what you're pulling out of the business, you're depleting somewhere.

This sounds basic. Most owners don't actually do it. They look at revenue. They look at the bank balance. They don't look at the line where operating profit is reported, and they couldn't tell you what it was last month.

**2. Separate the cash structurally.**

This is what Profit First does well. You set up separate bank accounts: operating expenses, owner pay, taxes, profit. Every dollar of revenue gets allocated to one of them by percentage as it comes in.

When you can only see what's in the operating account, you can't accidentally spend tax money. When profit goes into its own account, it accumulates. When you take an owner draw, it comes from the owner-pay account that's already been funded, not from the general pile.

This is structural, not behavioral. It works because it changes what you can see and touch, not because it changes your discipline.

## The honest hard part

The reason most owners haven't fixed this isn't ignorance. It's habit and identity. If you've been taking draws against revenue for ten years, the habit is sticky. If your household relies on those draws being a certain size, reducing them feels like personal failure.

The fix is to look at the math, see where you actually are, and adjust. Sometimes that means smaller draws for a quarter while you rebuild. Sometimes it means raising prices or cutting an unprofitable service line so the math actually works. Sometimes it means making peace with the fact that the business is currently funding a lifestyle it can't sustain.

The harder you avoid looking, the worse the math gets. The faster you look, the less catastrophic the fix.
