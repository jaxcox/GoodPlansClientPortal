# Goals too big, too far out

**URL:** `/blog/goals-too-big-too-far-out`
**Meta title:** Why "$5M in 5 Years" Isn't a Goal | The Good Plans Co
**Meta description:** Setting a five-year goal feels ambitious. It's also one of the easiest ways to avoid the next 90 days. Here's why goal-setting at the wrong altitude kills momentum.

---

## The big aspirational goal that never quite gets to "do"

Most owners I work with have a version of this goal:

> "I want to hit $5M in five years."

Or "I want to double the business in three years." Or "I want to be at $X in ten."

The goal sounds great. It feels ambitious. It has a target number and a timeline. It's the kind of thing you'd say at a networking event and people would nod.

And it's almost completely useless.

## Why big-and-far-out goals don't drive behavior

A five-year goal does one thing well: it announces an intention. It does almost nothing for the actual operating decisions you have to make this week.

If your goal is "$5M in five years" and you're at $1.8M right now, what does that tell you to do on Monday morning?

Hire someone? Maybe. Raise prices? Maybe. Add a service line? Maybe. Cut a customer? Maybe. Open a second location? Maybe. Spend more on marketing? Maybe.

The goal doesn't pick. Which means in practice, you don't pick either. You keep doing what you were doing.

Distant goals also have a sneaky psychological cost. They give you permission to feel like you're "working on" something big without doing anything specific. They become a story you tell yourself instead of a target you're moving toward.

The biggest tell that a goal is too big-and-far-out: when you check in on it once a year (or never), and the only feedback is "I'm not there yet."

## The right altitude

Goals work when they're close enough that this week's actions visibly move the needle.

For most operating businesses, the right altitude looks like:

- **Annual:** a clear revenue and profit target for the year, with explicit assumptions (number of jobs, average ticket, close rate, margin).
- **Quarterly:** a target for the next 90 days that lays out what each month needs to look like.
- **Monthly:** a number for the month, broken into weekly checkpoints.
- **Weekly:** specific actions that ladder up to the monthly number.

When you have all four, this week's behavior is shaped by the year. The big number isn't a story; it's a math problem that needs solving piece by piece.

## A specific example

Take an owner with an annual goal of $2.4M. With seasonality, that lands in twelve monthly chunks of varying size — let's say January at $150K, May at $220K, July at $250K, December at $180K, etc.

Today is Monday of the third week of May. The owner can ask:

- What was last week's revenue? ($48K)
- Where do I need to be by month-end to hit May's $220K? ($72K more in the next two weeks)
- What does that require? (24 jobs or about 12 per week, at an average ticket of $3K with current close rate)
- What does the leading indicator look like? (estimates out — 30 last week, so 24 closed is plausible)

Now the goal is operational. Every Monday for the next two weeks, the owner knows exactly what they're trying to do. Goal-related decisions are obvious.

Compare that to "I want $5M in five years." There's no Monday-morning translation. The goal can't help you.

## What "good" looks like in 90 days

Here's the test for whether a goal is at the right altitude:

- You can name the goal in one sentence.
- You can do the math in your head to know if you're on track this week.
- You'd make a different decision on Monday morning based on whether you're ahead or behind.
- The goal expires soon enough that you'll either hit it or miss it, and you'll know.

If your goal doesn't pass that test, it's probably too big or too far out. Or both.

## Big goals still have a place

This isn't a "don't have a five-year vision" argument. Vision is useful. It guides which 90-day goals you set in the first place. Wanting to build a $5M business shapes whether you take on contract work, where you invest, how you hire.

But vision and operating goal are different things. The vision is the destination. The operating goal is the next mile marker.

Most small business owners I see have a vision, no operating goal, and a confused sense of why the bank account isn't moving. Fix the operating goal, and the vision starts to actually materialize.
